\title{CANONCITE: A Multilingual, Multi-Tradition Benchmark for\\
Canonical-Citation Attribution and Abstention}

\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When a model cites a canonical source---a scripture verse, a constitutional
article---citing the \emph{wrong} unit is a distinct and more damaging failure
than citing nothing. Existing attribution benchmarks score whether a claim is
supported by a retrieved passage; they cannot detect that the model named
\textsc{Gita} 2.48 when the source was 2.47. We introduce \textbf{CANONCITE}, a
benchmark for \emph{exact-ID} citation attribution and abstention over ten
public-domain canonical corpora spanning four traditions and five scripts
(188{,}557 citable units), every question posed in English, Hindi and the
corpus's native script. Instantiating a six-system ladder, we show the
cross-lingual collapse is a \emph{retrieval-ranking} failure: on our two pilot corpora,
attribution F1 under non-English queries rises from 0.177 to 0.690 through
hybrid and reranked retrieval alone, with the gold unit retrieved but ranked at median 7--13. A joint
discriminative exact-ID selector then attains the lowest misattribution of any
system at two LLM calls per item---but a paired bootstrap shows this is
\emph{directional rather than established}: significance depends on the
resampling unit and on whether abstention is removed from the denominator.
Contrary to our hypothesis, its gain comes from eliminating \emph{implausible}
citations rather than resolving near misses. Human verification covers a
stratified 120-item sample ($\alpha$-MASI $0.991$, on a sample where annotators
approved 99\% of items).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation is now the default remedy for factual
hallucination, and a mature literature evaluates whether a generated claim is
\emph{supported} by the passages a system retrieved
\cite{gao2023alce,bohnet2022aqa,hu2025caqa}. That framing has a blind spot. In
domains organised around a fixed, publicly known identifier scheme---scripture
verses, statutes, constitutional articles, case law---the citation is not a
pointer to evidence but a \emph{claim about identity}. A model that answers a
question about non-attachment correctly but attributes it to
Bhagavad G\={\i}t\={a} 2.48 instead of 2.47 has produced a fluent,
well-supported, and false citation. Support-based metrics score it as a success:
the neighbouring verse is on the same theme and entails much the same content.

This is most consequential where canonical texts matter most and NLP resources
are thinnest: religious and legal corpora in Indian languages, queried in Hindi
or the text's own script. Yet Indic benchmarks evaluate \emph{understanding} via
MCQ \cite{verma2024milu,parambench2025,indicqa2024}, and the one prior line
exploiting fixed identifiers covers a single jurisdiction in a single language
\cite{ovcharov2026citation}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We claim neither the fixed-ID idea
\cite{ovcharov2026citation}, nor first religious verse-attribution
\cite{islamiceval2025}, nor Indian scripture knowledge
\cite{parambench2025,verma2024milu}. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{C1 --- Benchmark.} The first multilingual, multi-tradition,
multi-script canonical-citation attribution and abstention benchmark: ten
corpora, 188{,}557 citable units, 622 items, each posed in English, Hindi, and
native script. The \emph{cross-lingual, multi-script attribution axis} is the
load-bearing, genuinely unoccupied novelty (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \textbf{C2 --- Metric suite.} A corpus-agnostic exact-attribution harness
operating over normalised IDs, integrating the fixed-ID existence check
\cite{ovcharov2026citation} with the citation precision/recall paradigm
\cite{gao2023alce} and adding misattribution and abstention measures
(\S\ref{sec:metrics}).
\item \textbf{C3 --- A characterised selection rule (secondary).} A joint
discriminative exact-ID selector (E2) with the lowest misattribution of any
system we test, at two LLM calls per item. It leads on
misattribution in every condition we measure and is significantly better than the
SOTA per item on English queries, but it \emph{trails} that baseline on English
F1 (0.472 vs 0.554), which cites far more often; and cross-lingually the
advantage is not establishable at this scale, depending on the aggregation and
the resampling unit. Its advantage is also capacity-gated, reversing below a
threshold between 8B and 14B. We present E2 as a probe of \emph{why} residual
attribution errors are hard, not as a system we claim beats the state of the
art.
\item \textbf{C4 --- Findings.} The cross-lingual collapse is localised to
retrieval \emph{ranking} (the gold unit is retrieved but ranked at median 7--13);
the near-miss share of residual misattributions grows from 8\% to 13\% as systems
improve, reaching $1.8\times$ the measured chance rate while remaining a
minority; and MAR-exist $=0.000$
throughout separates ``cannot read the language'' from ``misattributes''.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Status of this release.} CANONCITE v1 ships model-drafted items whose
gold citations are sampled from, and automatically validated against, the closed
corpus ID space. Human verification covers a stratified sample of 120 items,
\textbf{double-annotated}, with Krippendorff's $\alpha$-MASI $=0.991$ on
citation sets and 118 items promoted to verified gold (\S\ref{sec:agreement}). All system
comparisons are \emph{relative} comparisons over a common, automatically
validated label set: the caveat applies identically to every system, so the
orderings are unaffected, while absolute values may shift.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Attribution benchmarks.} ALCE \cite{gao2023alce}, AQA
\cite{bohnet2022aqa}, and CAQA \cite{hu2025caqa} score whether a generated claim
is entailed by retrieved passages, typically via NLI, in English. Their unit of
truth is \emph{support}, not identity; a near-miss citation to a semantically
equivalent neighbour is scored correct. Surveys of evidence-based generation
\cite{evidencesurvey2025} confirm this framing dominates.

\paragraph{Fixed-ID attribution.} \citet{ovcharov2026citation} shows a closed
citation graph turns verification into an exact lookup, for US legal citations
in English. We adopt and credit the idea; our contribution is extending it
across traditions, scripts and query languages---a generality claim no prior
fixed-ID work makes.

\paragraph{Indic and religious NLP.} MILU \cite{verma2024milu}, ParamBench
\cite{parambench2025} and IndicQA \cite{indicqa2024} evaluate Indic
\emph{understanding} via MCQ; IL-TUR \cite{iltur2024} and ILDC
\cite{malik2021ildc} cover Indian legal reasoning without exact-citation
scoring. IslamicEval \cite{islamiceval2025} targets verse hallucination in one
tradition, monolingually. None measures exact-ID attribution across languages
and scripts.

\paragraph{Repair methods.} CiteFix \cite{maheshwari2025citefix}, VeriCite
\cite{qian2025vericite}, RARR \cite{gao2023rarr}, Self-RAG
\cite{asai2024selfrag} and CRAG \cite{yan2024crag} verify or repair per-passage;
we find the family largely subsumed once ranking is fixed.

\section{The CANONCITE Benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}

\paragraph{Corpora.} Ten public-domain canonical texts spanning Hindu, Buddhist,
Sikh and Christian traditions plus Indian constitutional law, in five scripts. Each corpus is frozen as a
\texttt{corpus\_index.jsonl} keyed by its own canonical identifier scheme
(\texttt{2.47} for G\={\i}t\={a} chapter.verse; \texttt{Art.~370} for the
Constitution), giving a closed ID space $U$ per corpus. Only public-domain
translations are released; where the only English translation is under copyright
(R\={a}m\={a}ya\d{n}a, Mah\={a}bh\={a}rata, Guru Granth Sahib), we release the
native-script text alone---an honest constraint that makes those corpora
\emph{require} cross-lingual retrieval.

\paragraph{Items.} 622 items across all ten corpora, each carrying a question, a
gold answer, gold citation ID(s) validated against $U$, near-miss distractors
(adjacent same-theme units), a question type, and an ambiguity label. Five
question types---\emph{factual}, \emph{retrieval}, \emph{conceptual},
\emph{interpretive}, \emph{unanswerable}---exercise different failure modes;
unanswerable items carry \texttt{must\_abstain} and no gold citation, so
abstention is measured rather than assumed. Every item is fully trilingual:
Hindi and native-script questions were produced with IndicTrans2
\cite{gala2023indictrans2} (P\={a}li by prompted generation, which IndicTrans2
does not cover). Gold citations are language-independent, so the three language
conditions are matched by construction---the comparison isolates query language
and nothing else.

\paragraph{Construction protocol.} A version-hashed
\texttt{corpus\_index.jsonl} per corpus fixes $U$; nothing downstream may cite an
ID outside it. Items are drafted by a pinned LLM \emph{conditioned on retrieved
real units}, over-generating $\sim$1.5$\times$; unanswerable items come from
out-of-corpus topics and cross-corpus swaps. Every drafted citation is validated
against $U$, so a fabricated identifier cannot enter by construction, and model
output is a draft that annotators confirm or correct against the open corpus.

\paragraph{Tiered annotation.} Annotator scarcity, not compute, is the binding
constraint, so we declare two tiers rather than hide a quality gradient: Tier A
(G\={\i}t\={a}, Bible, Constitution, Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l}) targets full double
annotation with per-citation support labels; Tier B covers auto-checkable item
types only, extending the language and tradition axes cheaply. Tiers are
recorded in the datasheet.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

All metrics operate over normalised IDs against $U$, so verification is exact
lookup with no NLI model. An item has gold set $G$; a system emits cited set
$C$.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Attribution P/R/F1 (exact)}: set overlap of $C$ and $G$, plus a
\emph{span} variant crediting a correct chapter with a wrong verse.
\item \textbf{Misattribution Rate (MAR)}: fraction of \emph{citing} items where
$C \not\subseteq G$, decomposing into \textbf{MAR-exist} (an ID not in $U$: a
fabricated identifier) and \textbf{MAR-support} (a real but wrong unit).
\item \textbf{Near-miss MAR (NMR)}: the share of wrong citations landing on a
declared distractor.
\item \textbf{Abstention accuracy}, \textbf{over-citation} and
\textbf{wrong-abstention}, over the \texttt{must\_abstain} items.
\end{itemize}

MAR-exist is 0.000 in every run we report: models cite \emph{real} units
incorrectly rather than inventing identifiers. This separates a base-competence
failure from an attribution failure and is why exact-ID scoring is informative
here.

\section{Systems and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{The ladder.} \textbf{A} naive BM25 RAG; \textbf{B} hybrid BM25+dense
RRF (BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3} + FAISS); \textbf{C} hybrid plus cross-encoder
reranking; \textbf{E} a binary per-candidate verify-and-repair layer, run as an ablation on
BM25 retrieval only and therefore absent from the system tables;
\textbf{D} an inference-time reproduction of Self-RAG
\cite{asai2024selfrag} + CRAG \cite{yan2024crag}, where a CRAG-style evaluator
labels each passage and a Self-RAG ISSUP critique keeps a citation only if the
passage supports it; and \textbf{E2} (ours), which presents all reranked
candidates \emph{jointly} and forces one exact-source choice or abstention---one
LLM call rather than up to $k$. Grid: systems $\times$ 10 corpora $\times$ query
language (en/hi/native) $=$ 28 cells per system. Primary reader
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct \cite{qwen2024}, self-hosted; all prompts, temperatures and
$k$ fixed and logged. We pre-committed to a decision gate: \emph{E2 must beat D
on cross-lingual MAR}.

\paragraph{The collapse is a ranking failure.} On the two pilot corpora System A
attains 0.719 attribution F1 under English queries and collapses to 0.177 under
Hindi/native queries, with misattribution at 0.757. A recall probe resolves the
cause: the gold unit \emph{is} retrieved under cross-lingual queries but ranked
at median 7--13, outside the reader's window. Fixing ranking alone---dense
hybrid retrieval, then cross-encoder reranking---lifts cross-lingual F1 to 0.626
and then 0.690 and cuts MAR from 0.757 to 0.196,
without touching the reader. Applying the binary verify-and-repair layer (E) to
BM25 retrieval barely moves cross-lingual F1 (0.177 $\to$ 0.173): a verifier can
only repair \emph{to} a unit retrieval actually surfaced, so this family is
subsumed once ranking is fixed.

Table~\ref{tab:recall} isolates the mechanism. Dense hybrid retrieval
\emph{finds} the gold unit almost always (R@50 $=$ 0.93--0.99) in every language,
but under cross-lingual queries ranks it at median 7--13, below the reader's
top-5 window; English queries place it at rank 1. The collapse is therefore
neither a coverage failure nor a reasoning failure. Two levers follow---widen $k$,
or rerank---and widening plateaus then hurts: a $k$-sweep of System E improves
through $k\!\approx\!10$ but \emph{regresses} at $k\!=\!20$ (G\={\i}t\={a} Hindi
$0.683\!\to\!0.670$) as the reader drowns in noisy passages. Reranking is the
right lever, and C@5 (0.690) beats E@10 (0.659) accordingly.

\paragraph{The English condition, where D looks best on F1.} The control column
of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} contains the largest system gap in this paper, and it
does not favour us: on English queries D reaches 0.554 attribution F1 against
E2's 0.472. We say so plainly, and then explain it, because the explanation is
the same mechanism this section is about. D cites on 67.8\% of English items to
E2's 51.1\%; F1 rewards attempting and penalises declining, so a system that
answers more often collects more correct citations---and more wrong ones. Scored
per item attempted, the ordering inverts and the margin is the clearest in the
paper: E2 makes \textbf{0.085} wrong citations per English item against D's
\textbf{0.153} and C's 0.141, a difference of $-0.068$ with a 95\% interval of
$[-0.123,-0.017]$ that excludes zero---the only E2-versus-D comparison in this
work that is significant under the coverage-corrected metric.

This also disposes of a rival reading of our whole method result. If E2's
advantage were merely a well-tuned decline threshold, it should pay off where the
reader is \emph{weakest}; instead it is largest and only significant where the
reader is \emph{strongest}. That is consistent with the capacity pattern reported
below and inconsistent with abstention-tuning as the explanation.

\paragraph{Reranking's advantage does not replicate at scale.} A second
disagreement between the pilot corpora and the full grid deserves the same
treatment. On the pilot, reranking clearly beats plain hybrid retrieval (C 0.690
vs B 0.626 cross-lingual F1), which is the basis for our claim that reranking is
the right lever. Across all ten corpora the ordering reverses---B 0.416 vs C
0.404 F1, and B 0.446 vs C 0.482 MAR---though neither difference is significant
(F1 $-0.012$, CI $[-0.076,+0.037]$; MAR $+0.036$, CI $[-0.067,+0.125]$). The
defensible claim is therefore narrower than the pilot suggests: \emph{ranking} is
the lever, and reranking is one way to fix ranking that is clearly better than
BM25 but not distinguishable from hybrid dense retrieval at scale.

\paragraph{Does E2 beat the baselines? A paired bootstrap.} Our margins are close
enough to measurement noise that point estimates cannot carry the claim, so we
test them, over \emph{all 18} cross-lingual cells. Two conventions matter and we
state them rather than let a subset do silent work. First, MAR is undefined when
a system cites nothing: C cited nothing under Hindi queries for Bible and the
Constitution, so macro comparisons involving C use the 16 cells where both
systems are defined, while E2-vs-D uses all 18. Second, those same cells are
perfectly \emph{defined} for wrong-citations-per-item---zero wrong citations over
$n$ items is $0.000$, the best possible score---so every per-item comparison uses
all 18. Excluding them would drop the two cells where the baseline scores
perfectly on the metric we introduced precisely to neutralise selectivity.
One consequence: each paired margin is computed on the cells common to that
\emph{pair}, so it will not in general equal the difference of the two headline
means. E2-vs-D uses all 18 and does reconcile ($0.387-0.443=-0.057$); E2-vs-C
uses 16, on which the means are 0.402 and 0.482 rather than 0.387 and 0.482.

\textbf{Directional, not robust.} E2 leads on point estimates under both metrics
(macro MAR 0.387 vs D 0.443, C 0.482; per item 0.139 vs C 0.154, D 0.166), but
significance depends on choices a reader could make differently. Under macro MAR
E2 beats both baselines; under the coverage-corrected metric the cluster
bootstrap finds nothing significant, while an item-level bootstrap finds E2-vs-D
significant ($[-0.051,-0.004]$) only by treating items as independent, ignoring
the clustering corpus and language induce. With 18 clusters the cluster
bootstrap is the conservative and appropriate choice, and we take its reading:
\textbf{E2 is consistently ahead and we cannot establish it at this scale.} We
apply no multiple-comparison correction over these six tests; the E2-vs-D macro
interval (upper bound $-0.002$) would not survive one.

\paragraph{The pre-committed gate, adjudicated.} We pre-specified that E2 must
beat D on cross-lingual MAR. \textbf{On its own terms it passes}: 0.387 versus
0.443, macro, which is the quantity we named. We nonetheless do not present that
as the paper's result, because the analysis above convinced us the pre-specified
metric was the wrong one---MAR's denominator counts only citing items, so it
credits a system for declining---and under the corrected metric the margin is
directional but not significant. Reporting the pass and stopping would have been
defensible by the letter of our own protocol and misleading in substance.

\paragraph{Aggregation, and abstention.} Two choices shape every margin, and we
state both. First, MAR is a per-cell ratio, so averaging cells (macro) weights a
12-item Gurmukhi condition like an 80-item Bible one, while pooling over citing
items (micro) weights by volume. It matters: the E2-versus-D cross-lingual margin
is $-0.057$ macro but $-0.011$ micro. We report macro, because each
corpus~$\times$~language condition is an experimental unit of equal interest and
pooling would let the two largest corpora settle a claim about cross-lingual
generality---while naming the coincidence that macro is also the
higher-variance estimator and the only aggregation under which our method result
clears zero. That is part of why we read conservatively throughout.

Second, MAR's denominator counts only \emph{citing} items, so a system that
declines the hard ones can post a low rate without discriminating better---and E2
has the highest wrong-abstention rate of the three (0.470 vs D 0.409, C 0.410).
We therefore also report wrong citations per \emph{item attempted},
$\sum_c \text{MAR}_c \cdot n^{\text{cite}}_c / \sum_c n_c$, whose denominator
counts all items including declined ones. Worked example (Yoga S\={u}tras Hindi):
$n=50$, $n^{\text{cite}}=38$, $\text{MAR}=0.132$, so $5$ wrong citations over 50
items $=0.100$. This pools over cells, so multiplying the macro MAR quoted
elsewhere by coverage will not reproduce it.

Cross-lingually E2 makes 0.139 wrong citations per item against C's 0.154 and D's
0.166, at citation coverages of 0.503, 0.448 and 0.556. Note what those coverages
say: \textbf{C, not E2, is the most selective system}, and C also beats D per
item---so selectivity and per-item accuracy do not track each other in the way
the abstention objection assumes. (C's coverage is 0.448 rather than the 0.519 of
the 16-cell subset precisely because the two cells where it cited nothing are
included here, as they must be.) E2 attempts fewer items than D but more than C,
and stays ahead of both once the advantage selectivity might confer is removed.
\paragraph{The correction reorders the baselines.} D leads C on macro MAR (0.443
vs 0.482) but trails it per item (0.166 vs 0.154): the reproduced Self-RAG/CRAG
pipeline emits \emph{more} wrong citations per item than the reranking baseline it
is built on top of, at five to eight times the LLM calls. Its lower MAR is bought
by citing more often, which MAR's denominator does not charge it for. Neither
margin is significant, so we claim no ordering---but the disagreement is a result
about the verify-and-repair family rather than a curiosity about metrics, and it
corroborates the E-on-BM25 ablation: once ranking is fixed, per-passage
verification adds cost without reliably adding accuracy.

\paragraph{The mechanism is not the one we hypothesised.} The near-miss rate says
so. NMR---the share of wrong citations landing on a declared near-miss
distractor---\emph{rises} as misattribution falls: cross-lingually, C sits at MAR
0.482 with NMR 0.082, E2 at MAR 0.387 with NMR 0.129 (on the pilot corpora,
0.259/0.121 versus 0.158/0.268). E2 therefore does \emph{not} preferentially resolve near misses; if anything the
opposite. But the share must be read at its actual size: at NMR 0.129,
\textbf{87\% of E2's wrong citations do not land on a declared distractor at
all}. Adjacency is a growing minority of the residual error, not its bulk, and
we say so rather than describe the residue as concentrating on near neighbours.
Two qualifications keep this from being over-read. The distractors are
\emph{model-declared} at item construction, so NMR measures adjacency as the
drafting model conceived it, not as a philologist would. A rising
\emph{share} is partly arithmetic once the easy errors are gone, though the
absolute near-miss rate also rises slightly ($0.031 \to 0.042$ on the pilot),
which is not automatic.

\textbf{Adjacency is over-represented, and we measure by how much.} A share is
only interpretable against the chance of hitting a distractor at all. We
therefore compute the base rate directly: over 228 pilot cross-lingual items, we
rerank the candidate pool as the systems do and ask what fraction of the
\emph{non-gold} slots in the top-8 are declared distractors. It is
\textbf{0.072}. Against that null, C's NMR of 0.082 is $1.1\times$ chance and not
distinguishable from it ($z=0.5$), whereas D's 0.115 is $1.6\times$ ($z=2.2$) and
E2's 0.129 is $\mathbf{1.8\times}$ ($z=2.7$). So adjacency errors are genuinely
over-represented among the residual errors of the stronger systems, and
increasingly so as systems improve---while still accounting for a minority of
them. The null assumes a wrong citation is uniform over non-gold candidates in the
reranked window, and the base rate is measured on pilot conditions and applied
throughout.

This also explains the capacity dependence reported below: narrowing to
the right neighbourhood is a judgement an 8B reader can partly make, but choosing
\emph{within} that neighbourhood is what scales with capacity. Consistent with
this, E2 has the best abstention accuracy of any system (0.955 cross-lingual,
0.986 English) and the lowest over-citation rate (0.045), but the highest
wrong-abstention rate (0.470, same basis)---it is the most willing to decline, which is the
correct bias for citation-critical use and a cost we state rather than hide.

\paragraph{Reader capacity, and how much of this is noise.} Swapping the reader
for Aya-Expanse-8B \cite{aya2024expanse} and gpt-oss-120B, holding retrieval and prompts fixed, the
E2-minus-D cross-lingual MAR margin on matched pilot cells is $-0.013$, $-0.027$
and $-0.081$ at 8B, 14B and 120B; over the 18 cross-lingual cells of the full grid the
8B ordering reverses outright (E2 0.604 vs D 0.577). Three points of four cells each, without
intervals: a direction, not an established trend.

To ask whether the 8B reversal is real, we replicated \emph{both} systems at that
reader end to end---all 28 cells each, identical configuration and quantisation.
Aggregates are stable across runs (E2 $0.604\to0.606$, D $0.577\to0.583$) with
per-cell $\sigma\!=\!0.030$ for E2 and $0.019$ for D, giving a paired 95\% band
of $\pm0.017$ on an 18-cell mean with nothing assumed. The reversal clears it:
the E2-minus-D margin is $+0.026$ and $+0.023$ across the two runs. One
transferable caution: an earlier estimate of ours taken from the closed-book
control gave $\sigma\!=\!0.077$, nearly three times either figure---a
retrieval-free control abstains far more, and abstention moves MAR
discontinuously. Noise should be measured on the system being reported.

\paragraph{Inference cost, in calls and in tokens.} The call counts are
structural, so they hold for any reader. E2 issues exactly \textbf{two} LLM calls
per item: one reader pass over the reranked candidates, and one joint selection
call. System D issues $k$ relevance judgements (one per retrieved passage), one
reader pass, then one to $k$ support critiques---at our $k=8$, between 10 and 17
calls, with 10 the floor. That is a $5$--$8\times$ difference in \emph{invocations}.

Invocations are the flattering unit, so we report the other one. E2's two calls
each carry \emph{all} $k$ candidates, whereas D's per-passage judgements carry
one each. Estimating prompt tokens from the templates and measured passage
lengths across five corpora, E2 uses $\approx$1{,}200 tokens per item against
D's $\approx$2{,}300---\textbf{$1.9\times$}, not $5$--$8\times$. Two qualifications: the token figure is estimated from templates, not metered;
and D's $k$ relevance judgements are batchable while E2's two calls are
sequential, so under a parallel implementation D's latency disadvantage largely
disappears and the argument for E2 is request count and tokens, not
wall-clock. Neither system uses an NLI model:
verification is an exact lookup against $U$.

\paragraph{Closed-book control: retrieval's contribution is real but wildly
uneven.} These are famous public-domain texts, so parametric recall is a live confound.
We therefore run all 28 cells with \emph{no retrieval}---the question asked
directly, the model citing from the closed space or abstaining---on the same
Qwen2.5-14B reader, matching the control to the grid it qualifies.

Closed book the reader attains 0.202 English / 0.150 cross-lingual F1 and
misattributes \emph{four citations in five} (MAR 0.807/0.841); retrieval adds
$+0.222$/$+0.254$ F1, so the results are not an aggregate memorisation
artefact.

Per corpus the picture splits sharply. The Bhagavad G\={\i}t\={a} is
substantially memorised (closed-book English F1 0.497 against 0.310 for the next
corpus), so its numbers are partly parametric---a caveat that matters because it
is one of our two pilot corpora. Four corpora (Bible, Guru Granth Sahib,
R\={a}m\={a}ya\d{n}a, Constitution) show retrieval adding essentially nothing
over memory because retrieval itself fails on them; where retrieval works it is
transformative (Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l} $0.086 \!\to\! 0.775$, Dhammapada
$0.182 \!\to\! 0.815$).

MAR-exist remains 0.000 unaided: even from memory the reader recalls
\emph{real} identifiers and assigns them wrongly rather than inventing them.

\section{Human Verification}
\label{sec:agreement}

To measure how far the automatic ID-space validation tracks human judgement, we
draw a deterministic stratified sample for double annotation: 120 items,
allocated proportionally across the seven corpora our annotators can read
(English/Hindi/Devan\={a}gar\={\i}), stratified by question type, with floors on
unanswerable (20) and near-miss-bearing (105) items. Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l}, Guru
Granth Sahib and Dhammapada are excluded for want of a script-competent
annotator---a stated limitation, not a silent omission.

\paragraph{Coverage and agreement.} The two annotators worked independently: the
review interface serves each only their own prior verdicts, so neither saw the
other's labels. One is an author of this paper; the other is not and has no
stake in the results. They agreed on the gold citation set for \textbf{119 of
120 items (99.2\%)} and on the verdict for 118 (98.3\%), giving Krippendorff's
$\alpha$-MASI $=\mathbf{0.991}$ \cite{krippendorff2011,passonneau2006masi}
(Table~\ref{tab:agreement}); adjudication promotes \textbf{118 items} to
verified gold.

Both disagreements are informative. On \texttt{gita-seed-0004} one annotator
approved the drafted G\={\i}t\={a} 4.8 while the other corrected it to 4.7---an
\emph{adjacent-verse near miss}, exactly the error class this benchmark exists to
measure. On \texttt{ys-seed-0047} they agreed on the citation (both empty) but
split on rejecting the item. That the single citation-level disagreement between
two humans is a near miss is itself evidence the phenomenon is real and hard,
not an artefact of automatic scoring.

\paragraph{Reporting $\kappa$ honestly.} Cohen's $\kappa$ on the verdict label is
$0.000$ despite 98.3\% agreement---the high-agreement/low-$\kappa$ paradox: 238
of 240 verdicts are \emph{approve}, so expected chance agreement nearly equals
observed and $\kappa$ collapses regardless of reliability. We report it rather
than suppress it, alongside PABAK $=0.967$ and raw agreement, which are the
interpretable statistics here; $\kappa$ is $1.000$ for question type and
ambiguity, where the marginals are not degenerate.

\paragraph{An automatic checker is a critic, not a cheap annotator.} Because a
second human is the scarce resource, we also ran an automatic pass over the same
items and measured \emph{human--model} agreement---not inter-annotator
agreement: machine verdicts are stored separately and excluded from
adjudication, so no item can be promoted to gold by a model. The result runs
opposite to the obvious one. A self-hosted Qwen2.5-14B agrees almost perfectly
(98.3\%/99.2\% exact match), which mainly means it is as willing to approve as
our annotators; a larger gpt-oss-120B agrees \emph{less} (91.8\%/93.9\%) and its
dissents are substantive objections both humans missed---it refused a Bible gold
set of three beatitudes because the question asked for all qualifying groups,
and argued Exodus 20:7 does not belong beside 20:3--4. Agreement with humans is
therefore the wrong objective for an automatic pass: the stronger model earns
its place as a \emph{screening} tool surfacing defects for adjudication, which
scales to the 502 unverified items where double annotation does not. On the
adjacency case our annotators split over, however, \emph{both} models sided with
the draft---so automation is unreliable for exactly the judgements this
benchmark targets.

\paragraph{Remaining limitations.} Coverage is 120 of 622 items (19.3\%),
concentrated in the seven corpora our annotators can read; the three excluded
scripts are unverified. Agreement this high on a sample drawn from
automatically validated items should be read as evidence that the ID-space
validation and the drafting protocol are sound, not as evidence that the
benchmark is error-free at scale.

One component is \emph{unmeasured} rather than partial, and we name it.
Validating the \emph{content-support} judge needs per-citation support ratings,
which our review interface does not collect---it records a verdict and an
optional citation edit. That calibration is therefore absent and is not
substitutable by the agreement above, which concerns citation \emph{identity}.
So the exact-attribution half of our metric contribution is human-validated on
this sample; the content-support half is implemented but not validated, and
closing it needs an interface change and another annotation pass, not compute.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}

Items are model-drafted; verification covers 120 of 622 (double-annotated,
$\alpha$-MASI $=0.991$), so absolute values may shift on the remainder though
orderings are unaffected. Three corpora---Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l}, Guru Granth
Sahib and Dhammapada---lack a script-competent annotator and are entirely
unverified. The 120B column
covers two corpora. All non-English questions are machine translated, and we
\emph{do not} bound that confound: with no natural-language queries anywhere,
``cross-lingual collapse'' and ``translationese collapse'' are not separated by
our design. Two observations bear on it without resolving it---the collapse is
localised to ranking rather than reading (Table~\ref{tab:recall}), and Devanagari
recovers to English-level attribution once ranking is fixed---neither of which is
what degraded query fluency would predict. A natively-authored query set for one
corpus would settle it and is the most valuable future addition.

\paragraph{Ethics.} All corpora are public domain and we release only
public-domain translations; where the sole English rendering is under copyright
we release native-script text alone. The benchmark measures \emph{attribution,
not endorsement}: citing a verse correctly is a claim about provenance and
carries no theological position, and no system here is intended to adjudicate
religious meaning. Because the corpora span four traditions, we report per-corpus
results without ranking traditions against one another, and the weakest
conditions (Gurmukhi, P\={a}li) reflect our own release and translation
constraints rather than any property of those texts. We document the release
following the datasheet convention of \citet{gebru2021datasheets}.

\paragraph{Availability and reproducibility.} Retrieval is local and
deterministic; reader temperature, prompts and $k$ are fixed and logged. Every
cell is checkpointed to JSONL and every number here is read from those files by
the harness rather than transcribed. Corpora, items, the 118 verified gold items,
the harness with its 45 tests and all systems are released together; released
text is public-domain, with three corpora shipping native script alone for
copyright. Machine annotations are stored apart from human verdicts and excluded
from adjudication, so no released gold item was promoted by a model.

\section{Conclusion}

Two results are solid. The cross-lingual attribution collapse is a \emph{ranking}
failure---retrieval finds the gold unit (R@50 0.93--0.99) and misranks it (median
7--13)---and a verify-and-repair layer cannot fix what retrieval never surfaced.
And the residual error is not what we expected: joint selection removes
implausible citations rather than adjacent ones, and near misses stay a minority
(13\%) of what remains, though at $1.8\times$ a directly measured chance rate.

Our method result is deliberately narrow. Joint discriminative exact-ID selection
attains the lowest misattribution of any system we test, at two LLM calls per
item, and leads on every point estimate---but a paired bootstrap does not
establish it: significance depends on the aggregation and on whether cells or
items are resampled, and the ordering reverses below a reader-capacity threshold
between 8B and 14B. We offer it as a characterised probe of why near-neighbour
attribution is hard, not as a system beating the state of the art. Settling that
needs more cells; settling how much of the cross-lingual effect is translationese
needs a natively-authored query set. We release the corpora, items, harness and
all system code.
