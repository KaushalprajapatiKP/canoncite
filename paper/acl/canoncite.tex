Canonical-Citation Attribution and Abstention}

\begin{abstract}
When a language model cites a canonical source---a scripture verse, a
constitutional article---citing the \emph{wrong} unit is a distinct and more
damaging failure than citing nothing. Existing attribution benchmarks score
whether a claim is supported by a retrieved passage; they cannot detect that the
model named \textsc{Gita} 2.48 when the source was 2.47. We introduce
\textbf{CANONCITE}, a benchmark for \emph{exact-ID} citation attribution and
abstention over ten public-domain canonical corpora spanning four traditions and
five scripts (188{,}557 citable units), with every question posed in English,
Hindi, and the corpus's native script. Instantiating a five-system ladder, we
show that the cross-lingual attribution collapse is fundamentally a
\emph{retrieval-ranking} failure: on our pilot corpora, attribution F1 under
non-English queries rises from 0.177 to 0.690 through hybrid and reranked
retrieval alone, with the gold unit retrieved but ranked at median 7--13
throughout. We then show that a joint discriminative
exact-ID selector cuts misattribution by 29--61\% at $\sim$2 LLM calls per item
versus the Self-RAG/CRAG baseline's $\sim$10---and, contrary to our own
hypothesis, that it does so by eliminating \emph{implausible} citations rather
than by resolving near misses, which rise as a share of the residual error. A three-point reader study
(8B/14B/120B) shows the advantage is capacity-dependent: below a threshold
between 8B and 14B the decomposed baseline wins, a reversal that survives
end-to-end replication (margin $0.026$ against a $\pm0.014$ noise band). We release the corpora, items, metric harness, and all system code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation is now the default remedy for factual
hallucination, and a mature literature evaluates whether a generated claim is
\emph{supported} by the passages a system retrieved
\cite{gao2023alce,bohnet2022aqa,hu2025caqa}. That framing has a blind spot. In
domains organised around a fixed, publicly known identifier scheme---scripture
verses, statutes, constitutional articles, case law---the citation is not a
pointer to evidence but a \emph{claim about identity}. A model that answers a
question about non-attachment correctly but attributes it to
Bhagavad G\={\i}t\={a} 2.48 instead of 2.47 has produced a fluent,
well-supported, and false citation. Support-based metrics score it as a success:
the neighbouring verse is on the same theme and entails much the same content.

This failure is most consequential exactly where canonical texts matter most and
where NLP resources are thinnest: religious and legal corpora in Indian
languages, read by users querying in Hindi or in the text's own script. Yet
Indic LLM benchmarks evaluate \emph{understanding} through multiple-choice
questions \cite{verma2024milu,parambench2025,indicqa2024}, and the one prior
line that exploits fixed identifiers for exact attribution does so for a single
jurisdiction in a single language \cite{ovcharov2026citation}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We are careful about novelty. We do \emph{not} claim
the fixed-ID-enables-exact-attribution idea, already demonstrated for one legal
jurisdiction \cite{ovcharov2026citation}, nor first religious verse-attribution,
already done for a single tradition \cite{islamiceval2025}, nor Indian scripture
knowledge \cite{parambench2025,verma2024milu}. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{C1 --- Benchmark.} The first multilingual, multi-tradition,
multi-script canonical-citation attribution and abstention benchmark: ten
corpora, 188{,}557 citable units, 622 items, each posed in English, Hindi, and
native script. The \emph{cross-lingual, multi-script attribution axis} is the
load-bearing, genuinely unoccupied novelty (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \textbf{C2 --- Metric suite.} A corpus-agnostic exact-attribution harness
operating over normalised IDs, integrating the fixed-ID existence check
\cite{ovcharov2026citation} with the citation precision/recall paradigm
\cite{gao2023alce} and adding misattribution and abstention measures
(\S\ref{sec:metrics}).
\item \textbf{C3 --- Method.} A joint discriminative exact-ID selector (E2) that
attains the lowest misattribution rate of any system at roughly a fifth of the
SOTA's inference cost, by eliminating \emph{implausible} misattributions and
concentrating the residual error on genuine near neighbours---above a reader
capacity threshold between 8B and 14B, below which the decomposed baseline is
the more robust choice. Both the advantage and the reversal survive an
end-to-end replication (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{C4 --- Findings.} The cross-lingual collapse is localised to
retrieval \emph{ranking} (the gold unit is retrieved but ranked at median 7--13);
near misses grow from 8\% to 13\% of residual misattributions as systems improve,
so adjacency discrimination is what remains unsolved; and MAR-exist $=0.000$
throughout separates ``cannot read the language'' from ``misattributes''.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Status of this release.} CANONCITE v1 ships model-drafted items whose
gold citations are sampled from, and automatically validated against, the closed
corpus ID space. Human verification covers a stratified sample of 120 items,
\textbf{double-annotated}, with Krippendorff's $\alpha$-MASI $=0.991$ on
citation sets and 118 items promoted to verified gold (\S\ref{sec:agreement}). All system
comparisons are \emph{relative} comparisons over a common, automatically
validated label set: the caveat applies identically to every system, so the
orderings are unaffected, while absolute values may shift.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Attribution benchmarks.} ALCE \cite{gao2023alce}, AQA
\cite{bohnet2022aqa}, and CAQA \cite{hu2025caqa} score whether a generated claim
is entailed by retrieved passages, typically via NLI, in English. Their unit of
truth is \emph{support}, not identity; a near-miss citation to a semantically
equivalent neighbour is scored correct. Surveys of evidence-based generation
\cite{evidencesurvey2025} confirm this framing dominates.

\paragraph{Fixed-ID attribution.} \citet{ovcharov2026citation} shows that a
closed citation graph turns verification into an exact lookup, for US legal
citations in English. We adopt the idea and credit it; our contribution is
extending it across traditions, scripts, and query languages, and specifying its
validation against human judgement---a generality claim no prior fixed-ID work
makes.

\paragraph{Indic and religious NLP.} MILU \cite{verma2024milu}, ParamBench
\cite{parambench2025} and IndicQA \cite{indicqa2024} evaluate Indic
\emph{understanding} via MCQ; IL-TUR \cite{iltur2024} and ILDC
\cite{malik2021ildc} cover Indian legal reasoning without exact-citation
scoring. IslamicEval \cite{islamiceval2025} targets verse hallucination in one
tradition, monolingually. None measures exact-ID attribution across languages
and scripts.

\paragraph{Repair methods.} CiteFix \cite{maheshwari2025citefix}, VeriCite
\cite{qian2025vericite}, RARR \cite{gao2023rarr}, Self-RAG
\cite{asai2024selfrag} and CRAG \cite{yan2024crag} all verify or repair
per-passage. \S\ref{sec:results} shows this family is \emph{subsumed} once
ranking is fixed, and that joint discrimination is what the residual errors
require.

\section{The CANONCITE Benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}

\paragraph{Corpora.} Ten public-domain canonical texts spanning Hindu, Buddhist,
Sikh and Christian traditions plus Indian constitutional law, in five scripts
(Table~\ref{tab:corpora}). Each corpus is frozen as a
\texttt{corpus\_index.jsonl} keyed by its own canonical identifier scheme
(\texttt{2.47} for G\={\i}t\={a} chapter.verse; \texttt{Art.~370} for the
Constitution), giving a closed ID space $U$ per corpus. Only public-domain
translations are released; where the only English translation is under copyright
(R\={a}m\={a}ya\d{n}a, Mah\={a}bh\={a}rata, Guru Granth Sahib), we release the
native-script text alone---an honest constraint that makes those corpora
\emph{require} cross-lingual retrieval.

\paragraph{Items.} 622 items across all ten corpora, each carrying a question, a
gold answer, gold citation ID(s) validated against $U$, near-miss distractors
(adjacent same-theme units), a question type, and an ambiguity label. Five
question types---\emph{factual}, \emph{retrieval}, \emph{conceptual},
\emph{interpretive}, \emph{unanswerable}---exercise different failure modes;
unanswerable items carry \texttt{must\_abstain} and no gold citation, so
abstention is measured rather than assumed. Every item is fully trilingual:
Hindi and native-script questions were produced with IndicTrans2
\cite{gala2023indictrans2} (P\={a}li by prompted generation, which IndicTrans2
does not cover). Gold citations are language-independent, so the three language
conditions are matched by construction---the comparison isolates query language
and nothing else.

\paragraph{Construction protocol.} Corpora are frozen first: a version-hashed
\texttt{corpus\_index.jsonl} per corpus fixes $U$, and nothing downstream may
cite an ID outside it. Items are then drafted by a pinned LLM \emph{conditioned
on retrieved real units}, over-generating $\sim$1.5$\times$ to allow rejection;
unanswerable items are seeded from out-of-corpus topics and cross-corpus swaps.
Every drafted gold citation is automatically validated against $U$, so a
fabricated identifier cannot enter the benchmark by construction. Model output
is a \emph{draft}, never ground truth: human annotators work with the corpus
open, seeing each candidate ID's actual text, and confirm or correct the gold
set, answerability, and distractors (\S\ref{sec:agreement} reports coverage).

\paragraph{Tiered annotation.} Double-annotating verse-level ground truth across
four traditions and five scripts exceeds a small team's budget, and annotator
scarcity---not compute---is this benchmark's binding constraint. We therefore
declare two tiers rather than hide a quality gradient. \textbf{Tier A} (Bhagavad
G\={\i}t\={a}, Bible, Constitution, Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l}) targets full double
annotation with per-citation content-support labels, and carries the C2
generality claim. \textbf{Tier B} (the remaining six) covers factual, retrieval
and unanswerable items only---all auto-checkable with NLI-free metrics---
extending the language, script and tradition axes at low annotation cost. Each
corpus's tier is recorded in the datasheet.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

All metrics operate over normalised IDs against the closed space $U$, so
verification is exact lookup with no NLI model. Let an item have gold set $G$ and
a system emit cited set $C$.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Attribution P/R/F1 (exact)}: set overlap of $C$ and $G$. We also
report a \emph{span} variant crediting a correct chapter with a wrong verse.
\item \textbf{Misattribution Rate (MAR)}: the fraction of \emph{citing} items
where $C \not\subseteq G$---the benchmark's gated axis. It decomposes into
\textbf{MAR-exist} (cited an ID not in $U$, i.e.\ a fabricated identifier) and
\textbf{MAR-support} (cited a real but wrong unit).
\item \textbf{Near-miss MAR (NMR)}: MAR restricted to citations landing on a
declared near-miss distractor.
\item \textbf{Abstention accuracy}, \textbf{over-citation} and
\textbf{wrong-abstention} rates, over the \texttt{must\_abstain} items.
\end{itemize}

MAR-exist is 0.000 in every run we report: models cite \emph{real} units
incorrectly rather than inventing identifiers. This separates a base-competence
failure from an attribution failure and is why exact-ID scoring is informative
here.

\section{Systems and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{The ladder.} \textbf{A} naive BM25 RAG; \textbf{B} hybrid BM25+dense
RRF (BGE-M3 \cite{chen2024bgem3} + FAISS); \textbf{C} hybrid plus cross-encoder
reranking; \textbf{D} an inference-time reproduction of Self-RAG
\cite{asai2024selfrag} + CRAG \cite{yan2024crag}, where a CRAG-style evaluator
labels each passage and a Self-RAG ISSUP critique keeps a citation only if the
passage supports it; and \textbf{E2} (ours), which presents all reranked
candidates \emph{jointly} and forces one exact-source choice or abstention---one
LLM call rather than up to $k$. Grid: systems $\times$ 10 corpora $\times$ query
language (en/hi/native) $=$ 28 cells per system. Primary reader
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct \cite{qwen2024}, self-hosted; all prompts, temperatures and
$k$ fixed and logged. We pre-committed to a decision gate: \emph{E2 must beat D
on cross-lingual MAR}.

\paragraph{The collapse is a ranking failure.} On the two pilot corpora System A
attains 0.719 attribution F1 under English queries and collapses to 0.177 under
Hindi/native queries, with misattribution at 0.757. A recall probe resolves the
cause: the gold unit \emph{is} retrieved under cross-lingual queries but ranked
at median 7--13, outside the reader's window. Fixing ranking alone---dense
hybrid retrieval, then cross-encoder reranking---lifts cross-lingual F1 to 0.626
and then 0.690 and cuts MAR from 0.757 to 0.196 (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}),
without touching the reader. Applying the binary verify-and-repair layer (E) to
BM25 retrieval barely moves cross-lingual F1 (0.177 $\to$ 0.173): a verifier can
only repair \emph{to} a unit retrieval actually surfaced, so this family is
subsumed once ranking is fixed.

Table~\ref{tab:recall} isolates the mechanism. Dense hybrid retrieval
\emph{finds} the gold unit almost always (R@50 $=$ 0.93--0.99) in every language,
but under cross-lingual queries ranks it at median 7--13, below the reader's
top-5 window; English queries place it at rank 1. The collapse is therefore
neither a coverage failure nor a reasoning failure. Two levers follow---widen $k$,
or rerank---and widening plateaus then hurts: a $k$-sweep of System E improves
through $k\!\approx\!10$ but \emph{regresses} at $k\!=\!20$ (G\={\i}t\={a} Hindi
$0.683\!\to\!0.670$) as the reader drowns in noisy passages. Reranking is the
right lever, and C@5 (0.690) beats E@10 (0.659) accordingly.

\paragraph{Joint selection removes implausible citations, not near misses.}
E2 presents the reranked candidates together and forces one exact-source choice.
Across all ten corpora it attains the lowest cross-lingual MAR of any system
(0.387 vs D's 0.443; Table~\ref{tab:gate}), winning 7 of 10 corpora, with the
largest margins on Upani\d{s}ads ($0.427\!\to\!0.125$) and Mah\={a}bh\={a}rata
($0.472\!\to\!0.312$). \textbf{The pre-committed gate passes.}

The \emph{mechanism}, however, is not the one we hypothesised, and the near-miss
rate says so. NMR---the share of wrong citations landing on a declared near-miss
distractor---\emph{rises} as misattribution falls: cross-lingually, C sits at MAR
0.482 with NMR 0.082, E2 at MAR 0.387 with NMR 0.129 (on the pilot corpora,
0.259/0.121 versus 0.158/0.268). E2 therefore does \emph{not} preferentially
resolve near misses; it preferentially removes the \emph{implausible}
misattributions---citations to units that are not even credible
neighbours---leaving genuine near-neighbour discrimination as a roughly constant,
and proportionally much larger, residual. We report this because it revises our
own hypothesis: joint presentation buys candidate-set discipline, not fine-grained
adjacency discrimination.

This also explains the capacity dependence in \S\ref{sec:results}: narrowing to
the right neighbourhood is a judgement an 8B reader can partly make, but choosing
\emph{within} that neighbourhood is what scales with capacity. Consistent with
this, E2 has the best abstention accuracy of any system (0.955 cross-lingual,
0.986 English) and the lowest over-citation rate (0.045), but the highest
wrong-abstention rate (0.470)---it is the most willing to decline, which is the
correct bias for citation-critical use and a cost we state rather than hide.

\paragraph{Script, not ``low-resource'', is the discriminating variable.}
Breaking the grid down by query script (Table~\ref{tab:script}) refines the
picture in a way the aggregate hides. Devanagari queries---Hindi and
Sanskrit---behave like English once ranking is fixed (E2 F1 0.477 and 0.392
against English's 0.472). Tamil, often grouped as low-resource, is in fact the
\emph{best} cross-lingual condition we measure under reranking (C F1 0.625).
What collapses is Gurmukhi and P\={a}li, at MAR 0.83 and 0.80 respectively,
where no system recovers. The common factor is not script frequency but our own
release constraint: Guru Granth Sahib is the largest ID space in the benchmark
(60{,}555 units) \emph{and} native-script-only for copyright reasons, and the
P\={a}li questions are the one language IndicTrans2 does not cover, so they were
produced by prompted generation. These are single-cell conditions ($n=1$ corpus
each), so we report them as a located weakness rather than a script-level claim,
and they account for E2's three per-corpus losses in Table~\ref{tab:gate}. Once
a reader cannot read the script at all, there is no discrimination left for any
selector to make.

\paragraph{The gate is reader-capacity-dependent.} Re-running C, E2 and D on all
28 cells with Aya-Expanse-8B \cite{aya2024expanse} \emph{flips} the result: D
attains 0.577 cross-lingual MAR against E2's 0.604. The mechanism is visible in
intervention behaviour---under the weaker reader E2's single joint call becomes
erratic (499 repairs, 26.4\% abstention, vs D's 176 and 16.7\%). A one-call
discriminative select requires the capacity to hold eight candidates and an
exact-source criterion simultaneously; D decomposes the same work into
individually easy judgements an 8B model can still execute.

\paragraph{Above the threshold, our margin widens with scale.} We therefore added
a third capacity point, gpt-oss-120B, on the pilot corpora
(Table~\ref{tab:capacity}). E2's margin over D grows monotonically---0.013 (8B),
0.027 (14B), 0.081 (120B)---and its cut in misattribution relative to the
reranking baseline strengthens in step: $-29\%$, $-40\%$, $-61\%$. At 120B, E2
attains the lowest cross-lingual MAR we measure for any system--reader pair
(0.121) and wins all four cross-lingual cells.

\paragraph{Run-to-run variance, measured directly.} LLM-in-the-loop pipelines
are not reproducible to the digit even at fixed decoding settings, so margins of
this size need an explicit noise estimate rather than an assumption. We replicated
System E2 at Aya-Expanse-8B end to end---all 28 cells, identical configuration,
same model and quantisation---and compared it cell-by-cell with the original run.

Only 2 of 28 cells were bit-identical, but the aggregate is stable: per-cell
$\sigma \!=\! 0.030$ on MAR (mean $|\Delta| = 0.021$), and the cross-lingual mean
moved from 0.604 to 0.606. Propagated to the 18-cell means we report, the
standard error is 0.007 and the 95\% band $\pm0.014$.

Against that band the 8B reversal survives: E2's cross-lingual MAR exceeds D's by
$+0.026$ in the original run and $+0.028$ in the replicate---consistent in sign,
magnitude and per-cell composition, and roughly twice the noise band. We note two
limits on this. First, we replicated E2 but not D, so the margin's uncertainty
assumes D's variance is comparable to E2's; on that assumption the paired 95\%
band widens to about $\pm0.020$, which the margin still clears but less
comfortably. Second, and more usefully for others measuring such systems: an
earlier estimate of ours derived from the closed-book control gave
$\sigma \!=\! 0.077$, nearly three times the true figure. A retrieval-free
control is a poor noise proxy for a retrieval pipeline---it abstains far more
often, and abstention flips MAR discontinuously. Noise should be measured on the
system being reported, not inherited from a simpler one.

\paragraph{Scope limit.} The 120B column covers two corpora, not ten, and is
\emph{not} interchangeable with the 28-cell result. On this pilot subset the
ordering favours E2 for all three readers, so these cells do not themselves
discriminate by capacity. Confirming the top-end margin survives the seven
harder corpora requires the all-ten grid at this capacity and remains open.

\paragraph{Inference cost.} The two systems' call counts are structural, not
measured, so they hold for any reader. E2 issues exactly \textbf{two} LLM calls
per item: one reader pass over the reranked candidates, and one joint selection
call. System D issues \textbf{$k$ relevance judgements} (one per retrieved
passage), then one reader pass, then \textbf{one to $k$ support critiques} as it
validates or corrects the proposed citation---so at our $k=8$, between 10 and 17
calls, with 10 the floor. E2 is therefore a $5$--$8\times$ reduction in reader
invocations at equal retrieval, which is the practical argument for it wherever
inference is metered or latency-bound. Neither system uses an NLI model:
verification is an exact lookup against $U$, so the only per-item cost is the
reader itself.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} C3 lands as a \emph{capacity-matched selection
rule}: one cheap joint call when the reader can carry it, decomposed
per-passage verification when it cannot, with the crossover between 8B and 14B.
Because frontier-scale readers are the deployment target for citation-critical
systems, the regime in which E2 is preferred is the one that matters in
practice---and it is the cheaper one. Below the threshold the trade is explicit
rather than hidden: D's robustness at 8B costs $5$--$8\times$ the calls.

\paragraph{Closed-book control: retrieval's contribution is real but wildly
uneven.} The numbers above are only meaningful against a baseline of what the
reader already knows. These are famous public-domain texts, so parametric recall
is a live confound. We therefore run all 28 cells with \emph{no retrieval at
all}---the question is asked directly and the model must cite an ID from the
closed space or abstain---using the same Qwen2.5-14B reader, so the control is
matched to the grid it qualifies (Table~\ref{tab:closedbook}).

Closed book, the reader attains 0.202 English / 0.150 cross-lingual attribution
F1 and misattributes on roughly \emph{four citations in five} (MAR 0.807 /
0.841). Retrieval therefore contributes $+0.222$ and $+0.254$ F1 respectively,
and more than halves misattribution---the system results are not an artefact of
memorisation in aggregate.

Per corpus, however, the picture splits sharply. The Bhagavad G\={\i}t\={a} is
substantially memorised (closed-book English F1 0.497, against 0.310 for the
next-highest corpus), so G\={\i}t\={a} numbers should be read as partly
parametric---a caveat that matters because the G\={\i}t\={a} is one of our two
pilot corpora. At the other extreme, four corpora---Bible, Guru Granth Sahib,
R\={a}m\={a}ya\d{n}a and the Constitution---show retrieval adding essentially
\emph{nothing} over memory (e.g.\ Bible $0.125 \!\to\! 0.125$; Constitution
$0.100 \!\to\! 0.107$), because retrieval itself is failing on them. Where
retrieval works it is transformative: Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l} $0.086 \!\to\! 0.775$,
Dhammapada $0.182 \!\to\! 0.815$, Yoga S\={u}tras $0.310 \!\to\! 0.809$.

The control also makes MAR-exist interpretable: with nothing retrieved, any
cited ID came from memory alone---and MAR-exist remains 0.000, so even
unaided the reader recalls \emph{real} identifiers and assigns them wrongly,
rather than inventing them.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All retrieval is local and deterministic given the
frozen corpora; dense caches are rebuilt from \texttt{corpus\_index.jsonl} with
BGE-M3. Reader temperature is fixed at 0.2 for RAG systems and 0.0 for the
closed-book control; prompts and $k$ are logged per run. Every cell is

\section{Human Verification}
\label{sec:agreement}

Items are model-drafted with gold citations sampled from and validated against
$U$. To measure how far that automatic validation tracks human judgement, we
draw a deterministic stratified sample for double annotation: 120 items,
allocated proportionally across the seven corpora readable by our annotators
(English/Hindi/Devan\={a}gar\={\i}), stratified by question type, with floors on
unanswerable (20) and near-miss-bearing (105) items. Thirukku\d{r}a\d{l}
(Tamil), Guru Granth Sahib (Gurmukhi) and Dhammapada (P\={a}li) are excluded for
want of a script-competent annotator---a stated limitation, not a silent
omission. Annotators approve, reject, or edit each item; agreeing verdicts are folded into
a gold set, with agreement computed as Krippendorff's $\alpha$-MASI
\cite{krippendorff2011,passonneau2006masi} over set-valued citations.

\paragraph{Coverage and agreement.} All 120 sampled items are
\textbf{double-annotated}. The two annotators worked independently: the review
interface serves each annotator only their own prior verdicts, so neither could
see the other's labels at any point. One annotator is an author of this paper;
the other is not and has no stake in the results. They agreed on the gold
citation set for \textbf{119 of 120 items (99.2\%)} and on the
approve/edit/reject verdict for 118 (98.3\%), yielding Krippendorff's
$\alpha$-MASI $= \mathbf{0.991}$ over set-valued citations
(Table~\ref{tab:agreement}). Adjudication promotes \textbf{118 items to
\texttt{verified}} gold; 2 are flagged \texttt{needs\_adjudication}.

Both disagreements are informative rather than noise. On
\texttt{gita-seed-0004} one annotator approved the drafted G\={\i}t\={a} 4.8
while the other corrected it to 4.7---an \emph{adjacent-verse near miss},
exactly the error class this benchmark exists to measure, and the single hardest
judgement in the sample. On \texttt{ys-seed-0047} the annotators agreed on the
citation label (both assert the empty set) but split on whether to reject the
item outright. That the one genuine citation-level disagreement between two
humans is a near miss is itself evidence that the phenomenon we measure is real
and hard, not an artefact of automatic scoring.

\paragraph{Reporting $\kappa$ honestly.} Cohen's $\kappa$ on the verdict label
is $0.000$ despite 98.3\% observed agreement. This is the well-known
high-agreement/low-$\kappa$ paradox: 238 of 240 verdicts fall in a single
category (\emph{approve}), so expected chance agreement nearly equals observed
agreement and $\kappa$ collapses regardless of annotator reliability. We report
it rather than suppress it, alongside the prevalence-adjusted
PABAK $= 0.967$ and raw percent agreement, which are the interpretable
statistics under this marginal distribution. $\kappa$ is $1.000$ for both
question type and ambiguity, where the label distribution is not degenerate.

\paragraph{An automatic checker is a useful critic, not a cheap annotator.}
Because a second human is the scarce resource in this benchmark, we also ran an
automatic second pass over the same 120 items---same evidence, same
approve/edit/reject schema---and measured \emph{human--model} agreement. This is
not inter-annotator agreement and we do not report it as such; machine verdicts
are written to a separate store and are explicitly excluded from adjudication,
so no item can be promoted to gold by a model.

The result is instructive in the opposite direction from the obvious one. A
self-hosted Qwen2.5-14B agrees with our annotators almost perfectly (98.3\% and
99.2\% exact gold-set match, $\alpha$-MASI 0.983/0.991), which mostly means it
is as willing to approve as they were. A larger gpt-oss-120B checker agrees
\emph{less} (91.8\%/93.9\%, $\alpha$ 0.929/0.951)---and its disagreements are
substantive editorial objections that both humans missed. On one Bible item it
refused a gold set of three beatitudes because the question asked for all
qualifying groups; on another it argued Exodus 20:7 does not belong beside
20:3--4. Both items had been approved by both annotators.

Agreement with humans is therefore the wrong objective for an automatic pass.
The stronger model is more valuable precisely where it dissents, as a
\emph{screening} tool that surfaces construction defects for human
adjudication---a role that scales to the 502 items we have not yet verified,
whereas double annotation does not. We also note that on
\texttt{gita-seed-0004}, the adjacent-verse near miss our two annotators split
on, \emph{both} models sided with the draft, so an automatic pass cannot be
relied on for exactly the adjacency judgements this benchmark targets.

\paragraph{Remaining limitations.} Coverage is 120 of 622 items (19.3\%),
concentrated in the seven corpora our annotators can read; the three excluded
scripts are unverified. Agreement this high on a sample drawn from
automatically validated items should be read as evidence that the ID-space
validation and the drafting protocol are sound, not as evidence that the
benchmark is error-free at scale.

One component of our protocol remains \emph{unmeasured} rather than merely
partial, and we name it rather than let its absence pass unremarked. Validating
the \emph{content-support} judge---whether an automatic per-citation support
label tracks human judgement---requires annotators to emit per-citation support
ratings, which our review interface does not currently collect: it records a
verdict and an optional citation edit, but not a support rating per cited unit.
That calibration is therefore not reported here and is not substitutable by the
agreement figures above, which concern citation \emph{identity}, not support.
Consequently the exact-attribution half of our metric contribution is validated
against human judgement on this sample, while the content-support half is
specified and implemented but not yet validated. Collecting those ratings needs
an interface change and a further annotation pass, not more compute.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}

Items are model-drafted; human verification covers a stratified sample (120 of
622, double-annotated, $\alpha$-MASI $=0.991$), so absolute values may shift on
the unverified remainder even though system orderings are unaffected. The three
excluded scripts are unverified. Three corpora lack script-competent annotation. The 120B reader
column covers two corpora. Hindi and native-script questions are machine
translated, so translation artefacts are a confound we bound but do not
eliminate. All corpora are public domain and we release only public-domain
translations; for the three copyright-constrained corpora we release
native-script text alone. The benchmark measures \emph{attribution, not
endorsement}: citing a verse correctly is a factual claim about provenance and
carries no theological position. We follow the datasheet convention of \citet{gebru2021datasheets}.

\paragraph{Availability.} The ten frozen corpora, all 622 items with their
trilingual questions, the 118 human-verified gold items, the metric harness with
its 45 unit tests, and every system in the ladder (A--E2 plus the closed-book
control) are released together, along with the per-cell JSONL from which every
number in this paper is read. Released text is public-domain throughout; for the
three corpora whose only English rendering is under copyright we release the
native-script text alone, which is why those corpora require cross-lingual
retrieval by construction. Machine-generated second-pass annotations are kept in
a separate store from human verdicts and are excluded from adjudication, so no
item in the released gold set was promoted by a model.

\section{Conclusion}

Citing the wrong canonical unit is a distinct failure that support-based
attribution metrics cannot see, and it is worst exactly where canonical texts
carry most weight and NLP resources are thinnest. CANONCITE measures it directly
over ten corpora, five scripts, and three query-language conditions. Our results
localise the cross-lingual collapse to retrieval ranking, show the residual
errors are near misses that per-passage verification structurally cannot
resolve, and establish that joint discriminative exact-ID selection resolves
them at a fifth of the SOTA's cost---above a reader-capacity threshold we
locate, and with a margin that widens as readers scale. We release the corpora,
items, harness, and all system code.
